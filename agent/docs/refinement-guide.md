# Commander Deck Exploration & Refinement Guide

## Purpose

This guide is for building Commander decks through iterative exploration without losing the deck’s actual identity. It is meant to guide future conversations from the first spark of an idea through a tight, functional 99.

The goal is not to build the mathematically strongest deck by default. The goal is to build a deck that reliably does the thing it was built to do, plays at the intended social/power level, and has enough structure that its fun cards actually get to matter.

---

# 1. Start with the thesis, not the commander

Before picking cards, define the deck’s emotional and mechanical thesis.

A good thesis has three parts:

1. **Experience**: What should the deck feel like at the table?
2. **Mechanism**: What game action creates that feeling?
3. **Boundary**: What does the deck deliberately avoid?

Example:

> “I want a political copy deck where opponents’ decks get more screen time. I want to copy or cast their cool things without making them feel stolen. I want to avoid hard theft, hand attacks, lock pieces, and generic Izzet spellslinger drift.”

This is much better than:

> “I want a clone deck.”

The thesis becomes the filter for every card.

## The commander test

A commander should pass at least two of these three tests:

- **Vibe match**: Does it communicate the deck’s social identity?
- **Mechanical lift**: Does it make the deck’s core actions stronger or more frequent?
- **Threat profile**: Does it let the deck function without making the table panic immediately?

A commander can be a mascot, an engine, or both. Be honest about which it is.

---

# 2. Split the process into four phases

Do not discover, verify, cut, and optimize all at once. That produces confident nonsense, which is humanity’s most persistent format.

## Phase A: Exploration

Goal: Find possibilities.

Be broad. Search by mechanics, vibes, rules text, known card families, and adjacent strategies.

Do not cut too early. The first phase is about seeing what exists.

Useful questions:

- What cards directly do the deck’s core thing?
- What cards do the same thing from weird angles?
- What cards turn the core thing into mana, cards, damage, protection, or inevitability?
- What cards make the deck more fun for opponents, not just more powerful for me?
- What cards look unrelated but exploit the same rules texture?

## Phase B: Verification

Goal: Stop lying to ourselves about what cards actually do.

Before cuts, create a card ledger with:

- Card name
- Mana cost / mana value
- Color identity
- Type
- Short functional summary
- Tags
- Role in the deck
- Failure mode

Important: Verify card text before doing serious analysis. Do not rely on memory, especially for new cards, alternate-universe reskins, split cards, modal double-faced cards, alchemy/Universes Beyond cards, or mechanics with weird templating.

If a card’s details are uncertain, mark it as uncertain instead of smoothing over the uncertainty. One wrong color, cost, or timing restriction can poison the whole cut analysis.

## Phase C: Structural build

Goal: Make sure the deck can actually play Magic.

Decide target slots before arguing about pet cards.

Typical Commander skeleton:

- **36–38 lands**
- **8–12 ramp / mana development pieces**
- **8–12 draw / card advantage pieces**
- **8–12 interaction pieces**
- **2–4 board wipes or reset buttons**, depending on deck style
- **2–5 protection pieces**, especially if commander-dependent
- **25–35 core theme cards**, depending on overlap

The best decks blur these categories. A card that is ramp plus theme is better than a generic rock. A card that is removal plus theme is better than a generic kill spell. But the jobs still need to be done.

## Phase D: Cuts and refinement

Goal: Make the deck coherent.

Cuts should come after the structure is known.

Good cut logic asks:

- Is this card core, support, payoff, glue, or indulgence?
- Does this card help the deck do its thesis, or is it just generically cool?
- Is this effect redundant with better versions?
- Is the card too expensive for what it contributes?
- Is it only good when the deck is already working?
- Does it pull the deck toward a different archetype?
- Is it a card I love because of one dream scenario, or because it performs consistently?

A painful cut is often a correct cut. If no cuts hurt, the list was not ambitious enough.

# 3. Use tags aggressively

Every card should have tags. Tags make hidden imbalances visible.

Common tags:

- **ENGINE**: Repeatedly drives the main plan
- **ENABLER**: Sets up the main plan
- **PAYOFF**: Rewards the main plan
- **RAMP**: Mana, treasures, cost reduction, cheating mana
- **DRAW**: Draw, selection, impulse access, repeatable card advantage
- **INTERACTION**: Removal, counterplay, redirection, bounce, combat disruption
- **PROTECTION**: Saves commander, board, or key engine
- **TOKEN**: Makes or uses tokens
- **CAST-FROM-EXILE**: Casts from exile or rewards it
- **GRAVEYARD-CAST**: Casts from graveyard or grants flashback
- **COPY-SPELL**: Copies instants/sorceries
- **COPY-PERMANENT**: Copies creatures/permanents
- **POLITICS**: Creates choices, bargains, shared resources, table incentives
- **CLOSER**: Helps end the game
- **LATE**: Seven-plus mana, or functionally late-game only
- **FLEX**: Replaceable, meta-dependent, or pet card

A card with multiple tags is more valuable than a card with one tag, assuming it is not too slow.

Example:

- **Cursed Mirror**: RAMP, COPY-PERMANENT, ETB-SYNERGY
- **Mystic Reflection**: INTERACTION, COPY-PERMANENT, TOKEN-SYNERGY
- **Storm-Kiln Artist**: RAMP, COPY-SPELL-PAYOFF, ENGINE

These are the cards that let a deck stay flavorful without collapsing.

# 4. Define what the deck wants to do by stage

A deck should not just have a theme. It should have a turn-shape.

## Early game: turns 1–3

The deck needs setup. Typical goals:

- Hit land drops
- Fix colors
- Develop ramp or cost reduction
- Play low-cost engines
- Start card flow
- Avoid looking like the archenemy too early
- Deploy cheap pieces that make future turns better

If a deck has too few relevant plays before turn four, it will feel like it is “full of fun cards” while doing nothing. This is not mysticism. It is a curve problem wearing a tiny hat.

## Mid game: turns 4–6

This is where the deck should begin doing its named thing.

Questions:

- Can the commander come down safely?
- Is there a follow-up play that immediately uses the commander?
- Does the deck have interaction while developing?
- Are the engine pieces cheap enough to deploy before the haymakers?

A commander deck often wins or loses coherence here. If the deck’s first meaningful themed play is turn seven, the list is probably too indulgent.

## Late game: turn 7+

Late-game cards should either:

- Create a memorable swing
- Rebuild from behind
- Close the game
- Produce a table story
- Turn earlier setup into payoff

Late cards are not automatically bad. But every expensive card must justify why it is better than another setup card, draw spell, protection piece, or cheaper version of the same effect.

A deck can support a few indulgent haymakers. It cannot support twelve of them unless the ramp package is brutally competent.

---

# 5. Curve analysis matters, but role curve matters more

Do not only count mana values. Count what the cards do at each mana value.

A healthy list needs:

- Cheap setup
- Cheap interaction
- Mid-cost engine pieces
- Mid-cost theme plays
- A small number of late payoffs

A pile of four-drops is a warning sign, even if every four-drop is cool. Four is where Commander decks go to become sluggish and self-important.

Look for overloaded spots:

- Too many 4–5 mana “value” cards
- Too many 6+ mana payoffs
- Too many cards that need the commander already in play
- Too many cards that are only good after another setup card
- Too many cards that do not affect the board before turn five

Then ask which cards are doing the same job less efficiently.

---

# 6. Mana base comes after the spell suite, but before final confidence

Do not build the mana base until the actual spell colors are known.

Once the 61-ish nonland cards are selected, count:

- Total colored pips
- Early colored pips, especially turns 1–3
- Double-pip requirements
- Commander requirements
- Utility land tolerance
- How many tapped lands the deck can survive
- Whether colorless utility lands are worth the cost

General Commander starting point:

- **37 lands** for midrange decks with multiple 5+ mana cards
- **36 lands** if curve is lower and ramp/draw are strong
- **38 lands** if curve is high, commander is expensive, or deck cannot afford missed land drops

Mana base principles:

- Blue-heavy decks need enough blue sources, not just “three-color lands.”
- Early red/white cards require actual early red/white sources.
- Utility lands must justify color strain.
- Colorless lands are expensive in three-color decks.
- Lands that double as spells are excellent if they do not wreck fixing.
- Fetch/shock/triome manabases are powerful but not always necessary.

A deck with beautiful spells and bad mana is just a theatrical mulligan simulator.

# 7. Prefer functional theme cards over generic staples

The best Commander refinement does not ask, “Can I fit Sol Ring and Rhystic Study?” like a tired algorithm.

It asks:

- What is the thematic version of ramp?
- What is the thematic version of draw?
- What is the thematic version of interaction?
- What is the thematic version of protection?

Examples:

- Ramp can be Treasures, cost reduction, cast-from-exile mana, creature-based rituals, or copy-sensitive mana engines.
- Draw can be magecraft, flashback value, opponents’ decks, shared exile pools, or political draw.
- Interaction can be transformation, redirection, copying, goad, phasing, bouncing, or symmetrical table choices.
- Protection can be blink, phase-out, indestructible, or effects that also populate/copy/retrigger.

Generic staples are allowed when they solve a real problem efficiently. But they should not be the default if a more thematic card does the same job well enough.

The rule:

> A slightly weaker card that does two on-theme jobs is often better than a stronger generic card that does one boring job.

# 8. Identify the deck’s traps

Every deck idea has gravitational traps. Name them early.

Common traps:

## The 62 Fun Cards Problem

The deck has too many exciting cards and not enough ramp, draw, interaction, or lands. It goldfishes beautifully and plays terribly.

Fix: assign slots before cuts.

## The Secret Different Deck Problem

A package pulls the list into a different archetype.

Examples:

- Copy politics becoming Izzet spellslinger
- Token copying becoming generic token doublers
- Graveyard recursion becoming blink-value control
- Opponent-card casting becoming theft tribal
- Friendly politics becoming group hug with no win plan

Fix: keep checking the thesis.

## The Win-More Engine Problem

A card is amazing only when the deck is already doing its thing.

Fix: distinguish enablers from payoffs. A deck needs both, but enablers come first.

## The Expensive Infrastructure Problem

A card claims to be ramp or setup but costs six or seven mana.

Fix: treat late setup as payoff, not infrastructure.

## The Memory-Based Card Error Problem

The builder misremembers cost, color, timing, or exact text, then makes confident decisions from bad data.

Fix: verify before analysis. Write card data down.

## The Pet Card Fog Problem

A card stays because of one imagined perfect moment.

Fix: ask for floor, average case, and ceiling. If only the ceiling is good, the card is probably cuttable.

# 9. Search better

Card search should evolve with the deck.

## Start broad

Search mechanics and vibes:

- “copy target creature”
- “cast from exile”
- “opponent’s library”
- “demonstrate”
- “populate”
- “legend rule doesn’t apply”
- “whenever you copy a spell”
- “cards you don’t own”

## Then search exact rules text

When the deck’s need becomes precise, search exact phrases:

- “you may cast that card”
- “you may play that card”
- “from exile”
- “from an opponent’s library”
- “target creature you control”
- “token that’s a copy”
- “not legendary”
- “gains flashback”
- “cast from your graveyard”
- “first spell you cast from anywhere other than your hand”

## Search exclusions

Exclude the wrong category when needed:

- Not instants/sorceries only
- Not putting onto battlefield
- Not stealing from battlefield
- Not random cascade/discover
- Not black if deck is Jeskai
- Not generic impulse draw if the deck needs specific cards

## Search by rules texture

Some of the best cards are not obvious theme cards. Search for what the deck’s objects become:

- Tokens
- Same-name permanents
- Legendary copies
- Spells cast from exile
- Spells copied but not cast
- Cards owned by opponents
- Cards cast from graveyard
- Permanents entering as copies

This finds cards that exploit the deck’s actual mechanics, not just its surface theme.

# 10. Cut by role, not by affection

When cutting, create tiers.

## Tier 1: Core identity

These cards are the deck. Cutting them changes the premise.

Protect these unless the premise changes.

## Tier 2: Functional engines

These make the deck operate.

Ramp, draw, repeatable card access, cost reduction, protection, and interaction that match the theme.

Do not overcut this tier for more fun cards. That is how decks become museum exhibits.

## Tier 3: Payoffs

These reward the deck for doing its thing.

Keep the best, cut redundant or expensive versions.

## Tier 4: Redundant role-players

These do the right thing, but less efficiently or less uniquely than other cards.

Most cuts should come from here.

## Tier 5: Cute but unsupported

These are cards that belong in a neighboring deck.

Cut them ruthlessly. Wish them well. They had a good audition.

# 11. Document the reasoning

A good deckbuilding conversation should leave behind a paper trail.

For every major cut or include, write the reason in one sentence.

Bad:

> “Cut this because it is worse.”

Good:

> “Cut Wand of Wonder because it only casts instants/sorceries, while the deck’s bottleneck is casting opponent creatures from exile to trigger demonstrate.”

This prevents future self from re-adding cards for the same mistaken reason.

Documentation should include:

- Current thesis
- Commander rationale
- Deck skeleton counts
- Key engines
- Required categories
- Current cut list
- Watchlist cards
- Mana base assumptions
- Cards intentionally excluded
- Reasons for painful cuts

---

# 12. Evaluate after building

After goldfishing or playing, track a few specific questions.

Do not just ask “did it win?” Commander deck testing based only on wins is how people become insufferable at kitchen tables.

Track:

1. Did I hit my land drops through turn four?
2. Did I have meaningful plays before turn four?
3. Did the commander matter?
4. Did I do the deck’s thesis at least once?
5. Did I run out of cards?
6. Did I have interaction when someone became scary?
7. Did my late-game cards close or just decorate the battlefield?
8. Did opponents seem involved or merely victimized?
9. Which cards sat dead in hand?
10. Which cards overperformed despite looking modest?

Use actual games to update the guide. Do not protect the first version out of pride. Pride has ruined more decks than mana screw.

---

# 13. Longevity check for this guide

This guide should remain useful because it is not tied to one deck, one commander, or one format trend.

The durable principles are:

- Start from thesis
- Verify card data before analysis
- Separate discovery from cuts
- Use tags and roles
- Build functional skeletons
- Protect early-game structure
- Prefer multi-role theme cards
- Cut redundant expensive effects
- Build mana after spell selection
- Document reasoning
- Test against the actual play experience, not just goldfish fantasies

The parts most likely to change over time:

- Default ramp/draw/interaction counts may shift with power level
- New sets may create better role-players
- Commander tables may change tolerance for certain mechanics
- Specific card evaluations may change as better versions are printed

The process should stay stable even when the card pool changes.

---

# 14. Refined operating instructions for future deckbuilding conversations

When helping build or refine a Commander deck, follow this order:

1. **State the deck thesis** in one or two sentences.
2. **Clarify constraints**: colors, budget, power level, social contract, must-includes, must-avoids.
3. **Separate exploration from evaluation**.
4. **Verify exact card data** before making cuts or legality claims.
5. **Build a card ledger** with cost, color, role, tags, and failure mode.
6. **Identify the deck’s true engines**, not just its theme words.
7. **Set target counts** for lands, ramp, draw, interaction, protection, and theme cards.
8. **Analyze the curve by role**, not just mana value.
9. **Cut from redundancy, slowness, off-plan packages, and low-floor pet cards**.
10. **Build the mana base after finalizing the spell suite**.
11. **Document why each cut happened**.
12. **Create a watchlist**, because some decisions require games.
13. **After testing, refine based on observed failure modes**, not vibes alone.

The central discipline:

> The deck’s fun cards deserve a structure that lets them happen.

That is the whole game. Well, that and pretending 100-card singleton is a reasonable way for adults to spend money.
